Practice or Klicky?

Students learn the LMS as they go, so later tasks are easier anyway (a practice or learning effect). Comparing students who met Klicky first with those who met it second shows how much is practice and how much is Klicky. Each line is the median own work at each task position; the dot colour shows whether that task was with or without Klicky.

  • Students who met Klicky second got faster from task 1 to task 2 on their own (67.7 s to 30.9 s): that is practice, learning the LMS layout (course tabs at the top, the left menu).
  • Only 1 student(s) met Klicky first (P20): own work with Klicky 16.4 s, later without it 50.3 s. Too few to separate practice from Klicky; with every student meeting Klicky second, part of the improvement is practice.

Cognitive load: how much thinking it took

Hesitation and stopping are signs of mental effort: the longer a student waits before acting, the more often the pointer stops and the longer it rests before a click, the harder they are working out what to do (Grimes & Valacich 2015). Klicky's own turns are left out.

Cognitive offloading: searching handed over to Klicky

When Klicky shows the way, the student no longer searches the screen: the pointer travels less and more directly, changes direction less, moves in fewer stop-and-go bursts and faster (load slows movement and adds corrections: Grimes & Valacich 2015; Thorpe et al. 2022; MacKenzie et al. 2001).

Navigation and disorientation: finding the way through the LMS

How directly the student reached the goal. Lostness (Smith 1996) combines pages visited, different pages and the fewest pages needed: 0 is a direct route, near 1 is lost. Navigation efficiency is the clicks needed divided by the clicks made.

Against an expert, and frustration

The Keystroke-Level Model (Card, Moran & Newell) predicts an expert needs about 2.65 s per click (think 1.35 s, point 1.1 s, click 0.2 s); the chart shows how many times slower the student's own work was. Rage clicks (3+ quick clicks on one spot) show frustration.
